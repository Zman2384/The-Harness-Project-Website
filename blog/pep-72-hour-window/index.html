The 72-hour window: what to know about PEP

CZChristopher ZacharieSeptember 14, 2026 — 2 min read — The 1 a.m. Reality
The 1 a.m. Reality1:04 a.m.

You feel it, or you notice afterward: the condom broke. Your heart is pounding, and your search bar is filling with worst-case results.

Panic says wait until morning and hope. That's the one move that actually costs you.

The pivotMATCH Framework

What PEP is

PEP (post-exposure prophylaxis) is HIV medicine taken after a possible exposure to keep the virus from taking hold. It's a short course, usually 28 days.

Start within 72 hours. PEP works best the sooner it starts. After 72 hours it's generally not recommended.

Your next three moves

  • Go now. An emergency room is open every hour of every day. Any ER can and should provide PEP — don’t wait for a clinic appointment.
  • Say it plainly. Use the script below.
  • Book follow-up testing before you leave.
"I had a possible HIV exposure and I need PEP."

PEP is a backup, not a plan

If you've needed PEP more than once, talk with a provider about PrEP — HIV prevention that works before an exposure happens.

Map it to MATCH

  • Medications: know PEP exists and where the nearest 24-hour ER is.
  • Awareness: schedule follow-up testing.
  • Tools: check condom fit, storage, and expiration dates, and use lube.
  • Communication: tell your partner what happened and what you're doing about it.
  • Habits: save the ER address in your phone today.
Key takeaway

A failed method isn't a failed plan. The clock is your cue to act, not freeze.

Bring it home

Every protection plan has a failure mode. The people who stay protected aren't the ones who never face 1 a.m. They're the ones who already know their next step.
